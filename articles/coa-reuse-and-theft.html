COA Reuse and Theft, Explained

The core idea: a Certificate of Analysis proves a test happened on some sample — not that it came from your vial, nor that the vendor showing it is the one who ordered it. That gap is exploited two ways: reuse and theft. A "real" PDF defeats neither.

Reuse

A supplier tests one good batch and gets a genuine COA. Then thousands of vials — many never tested — are sold against that single report. Every reseller buying from that supplier can show the same document, even though your specific vial was never analyzed. One test, an ocean of untested product riding on it.

Theft

A vendor downloads someone else's genuine COA — a competitor's, or their supplier's — and presents it as proof of their product. The document is authentic; it's just not theirs. Posting a real PDF only defeats forgery. It does nothing against a stolen-but-real report.

Why "the PDF is real" isn't enough

People check whether a COA looks legitimate and stop there. But authenticity is the floor, not the ceiling. The real questions are: whose sample was tested, and does the result tie to this vendor and this batch?

How to defeat both

  • Insist the COA verifies on the lab's own portal, with the vendor named as the client.
  • Prefer independent blind testing — where a buyer purchases off-the-shelf and submits it, so the vendor can't choose the sample.

How reuse happens without anyone lying

A synthesis house produces a large batch and tests it once, properly, at an independent laboratory. That report is genuine. The batch is sold in portions to several resellers, each of whom fills vials under its own label and each of whom shows the same report, because it is the only report there is. Nobody has forged anything. Yet the report now stands behind thousands of vials filled in several places under different conditions, and any of those vials may have been contaminated, mislabelled or degraded after the sample was measured. Reuse is the default state of a report that has not been tied to a batch; theft is the exception. Both leave the buyer in the same position.

How theft is done, and how it is caught

A vendor downloads a competitor's genuine report, edits the client name and sometimes the batch, and posts it. The edit is invisible in the PDF. It is visible on the laboratory's portal, because the laboratory's record still names the original client and the original batch, and a lookup returns the unedited version. This is the whole reason the location of a report matters more than its appearance: theft is undetectable in a file and trivially detectable in a lookup.

Three questions that separate the cases

Whose name is in the client field on the laboratory's record? The vendor's: it commissioned the test. A supplier's: the vendor is showing an upstream report, which may be reuse. A competitor's: theft. Does the batch on the record match a batch the vendor is shipping? Yes: the report is about your product. No: it is about something else. How many products does the vendor show this one report under? One: normal. Several: reuse, and the report describes at most one of them.

What a vendor can do about reuse, and whether it does

The remedy for reuse is simple: test your own batch after filling, under your own name, and publish the result. It costs a few hundred dollars per batch. A vendor that resells upstream material and tests its own fills has closed the gap; a vendor that passes along the supplier's report has not, and the two are indistinguishable from the PDF alone. The client field on the laboratory's record is where they differ.

A note on genuine shared sources

Two vendors showing the same report may simply share a supplier and be honest about it. That is the benign case, and it is still not evidence about either vendor's fills. The distinction that matters to a buyer is not honest versus dishonest; it is tested-after-filling versus not.
