## Why does a Certificate of Analysis prove less than people think?

Here's what most people get wrong about COAs. A Certificate of Analysis proves that a test was performed on *some* sample. It does not prove that the sample came from your vial, and it does not prove that the company posting it is the company that ordered the test.

That gap opens two doors. The first is **reuse** — one good vial gets tested once, and thousands of units are sold against that single document. The second is **theft** — a vendor posts a genuine COA that belongs to someone else entirely, often their own upstream supplier. In both cases the PDF is completely real. Checking that a document isn't forged only defeats forgery, which was never the main problem.

The founder of one of the largest independent peptide labs has said this plainly: they have no way to verify the origin of the sample. That is not a criticism of the lab. It is an honest description of what a lab can and cannot certify — and it is exactly why a ladder of proof exists instead of a single yes-or-no answer.

## What are the five levels of proof, exactly?

Think of this as a ladder. Each rung closes a gap the rung below it leaves open.

| Level | What it is | What it still doesn't rule out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Weakest** | Vendor posts a COA PDF on their site | Reuse, theft, wrong batch, forgery |
| **2.** | The COA resolves on the *lab's own* verification portal | Reuse, theft, wrong batch (forgery ruled out) |
| **3.** | Client name on the COA is the vendor, and the lot on your vial matches | Reuse across a large run |
| **4.** | Independent blind testing — customers buy off the shelf and submit anonymously | Little; the vendor can't select the sample |
| **5. Strongest** | You send your own vial to a lab yourself | Nothing about origin — you controlled it |

Notice what changes as you climb. It isn't the quality of the science — the analytical method may be identical at every level. What changes is **who chose the sample**. That single variable is the whole ballgame.

## Which level do you actually have right now?

Pick a product you're considering and walk these in order. It takes a few minutes and it is the most useful thing you can do before spending anything.

- **Find the lab's name on the COA**, then go to that lab's own website — not a link on the vendor's page — and look for a verification or lookup portal.

- **Enter the report or accession number.** If it resolves to a server-rendered record, you've cleared level two. If the portal returns "OK" for any number you type, that portal proves nothing.

- **Read the client field.** If the client is a Chinese trading company rather than the vendor, you're looking at a raw-material COA, not a finished-vial test.

- **Match the lot number** printed on the physical vial against the lot on the COA when it arrives. A mismatch means the document describes a different batch than the one in your hand.

- **Check whether the vendor appears in any community blind-testing database.** Absence isn't damning — newer vendors won't be listed — but presence is a genuinely strong signal.

## Is it worth paying to test your own vial?

Level five costs real money: sending a vial to an established independent lab runs roughly **$60–90** per sample. That sounds like a lot until you set it against the order it protects. On a multi-hundred-dollar order, a one-time test is a modest fraction of the spend, and it is the only route where you personally control the chain of custody from vial to instrument.

My honest read: most people don't need level five for every purchase. It makes the most sense the first time you try a new vendor, or when the order size is large enough that being wrong is expensive. For everything after that, levels two and three — a portal-resolving COA with a matching lot — is a reasonable place to live.
