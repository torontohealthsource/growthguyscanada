Judge the operation by what it commits to in writing

Before any laboratory document enters the picture, a seller has already told you a great deal through its published policies. Does it state where it ships and where it will not? Does it say what happens when something arrives wrong, in terms specific enough to be held to? Is the returns position stated plainly, even when the answer is unfavourable?

A policy that is unfavourable but explicit is a better sign than one that is vague and warm. "All sales are final" is a real commitment you can price into the decision. "Customer satisfaction is our priority" commits to nothing and cannot be breached, which is precisely why it is everywhere.

The questions that reveal the most, and what the answers look like

Ask something specific and mildly inconvenient before you order. Which batch will ship for this product? Can you send the report for that batch? What happens if the vial arrives broken? These are ordinary questions with short answers if the operation is what it claims, and they are slow and evasive if it is not.

The content of the reply matters less than its shape. A seller testing per production run can name your batch, because the document already exists. One that tested once and has been reusing the certificate cannot, and will answer a different question than the one you asked — usually by restating a general quality claim. That substitution is the signal.

Price, stock and the shape of the catalogue

Pricing far below the rest of the market is information, not a bargain. Independent assay, cold-chain handling and per-batch documentation all cost money, and a seller substantially cheaper than everyone else has either found an efficiency or stopped paying for one of those things. It is worth knowing which.

Honest stock reporting is an underrated tell in the same family. A catalogue where nothing is ever out of stock, across dozens of items, is describing a supply chain that does not exist. Visible out-of-stock listings and visible batch turnover are small signs that what you are reading reflects a real inventory rather than a storefront.

Two errors to avoid in both directions

The first is treating presentation as evidence. A polished site, a professional-looking certificate and a confident tone take the least effort to produce of anything on this list, and they correlate with nothing. The second is treating any single gap as proof of bad faith: a missing report on a newly listed product is far more often a documentation lag than a concealment.

What survives both errors is a short list of things that are checkable rather than impressive: a stated shipping route you can confirm on your first tracking number, a testing claim you can verify on a laboratory's own domain, a batch code that matches, and policies specific enough to be wrong. Weigh those and discount the rest.

Time is the one test that cannot be faked

Most of the checks above can be completed in an afternoon, which is their strength and also their limit — a storefront built last month can pass all of them. The signal that is genuinely hard to fabricate is a record that extends backwards: batch numbers advancing over months, reports dated across a long period, products visibly moving in and out of stock.

This is the one test that costs an operator real time rather than real money, which is why it is worth weighting heavily. You do not need to wait yourself; you need to look at whether the published record shows a history. A set of documents all dated within a few weeks of each other describes a launch, not a track record, and there is nothing wrong with a launch so long as you know that is what you are buying into.
