Why the weak results are the strongest part of the record

A published set with no low values has usually been filtered rather than achieved, and filtering cannot be detected from inside the set — you cannot see what was removed by examining what remains. Synthesis, purification, handling and storage all move a purity figure, and a catalogue of over a hundred products tested repeatedly will produce a spread rather than a flat line near the top.

So the 33 results under 98%, left visible alongside the rest, are what make the 99.03% mean worth anything. An average computed over a curated subset is an average of someone's editorial choices. This one is computed over everything that was published, including the figures that do not flatter.

The part that is independently checkable

Counts are only as good as their source, and this is where most vendor testing claims fall down. Each report here opens on the testing laboratory's own portal, under the laboratory's reference, naming the client and the batch — rather than existing as a PDF served by the seller, which the seller can edit.

That is the difference between evidence and a picture of evidence, and you can confirm it on a single purchase. Take the batch code printed on the vial, open the report on the lab's domain, and check that the batch, the compound and the client all match. If it will not resolve there, the figure attached to it is a claim rather than a result.

Testing per batch, not per product

Of the 240 reports, 238 carry a batch code, and those resolve to 229 distinct codes. A near one-to-one ratio of codes to reports means testing is happening per production run rather than once per product line — which is the difference between a catalogue that is tested and a catalogue that has a certificate.

Repeated testing of the same item over time is what lets a buyer see whether quality held, rather than whether it was acceptable once, and it is the more expensive thing for a seller to do.
