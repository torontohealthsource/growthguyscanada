What Are Peptides? A Beginner's Guide (Canada 2026)

What is a peptide, in one line? A short chain of amino acids — the same building blocks proteins are made of, just fewer of them (typically 2 to 50), linked together by peptide bonds. That's a structural definition, not a claim about what any specific peptide does.

Peptides vs. proteins

The distinction is a rough convention, not a hard scientific line: shorter amino-acid chains are usually called peptides, longer ones that often fold into complex 3D shapes are called proteins. Insulin, for example, is sometimes described as one of the largest peptides or one of the smallest proteins, depending on which textbook you read — the boundary is fuzzy by design.

Research peptides vs. pharmaceutical peptides

The word "peptide" alone tells you nothing about legal or regulatory status. Some peptides are approved medications — insulin and the GLP-1 class of drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide) are peptides that went through full drug approval. Others are sold as research chemicals, labelled "for research use only," without that approval process. The difference is in the specific compound and how it's sold, not in the category "peptide" itself.
