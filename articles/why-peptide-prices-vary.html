Short answer: Price gaps between vendors usually reflect testing, sourcing, and shipping — not a deal. The cheapest vial is often the untested one.

What you're actually paying for

Independent testing costs money. Domestic, reliable shipping costs money. A vendor that does neither can always undercut one that does.

Why cheapest is a warning

When a price is far below everyone else, ask what got cut to get there. Usually it's the part you can't see on arrival — the testing.

How to value-compare

Compare price against verifiable testing, not against price alone. Buying tested product once beats buying cheap product twice.

What is actually in the price

A vial's price is the sum of five things, and vendors differ on all five. The raw material, bought in bulk from a synthesis house and priced per milligram by purity grade. The fill: lyophilising into vials, capping, labelling, which is a fixed cost per unit that matters more at small volumes. Testing, if any: a third-party report per batch costs a few hundred dollars and is spread across however many vials the batch holds, so a vendor running large batches pays cents per vial for it and a vendor running small batches pays dollars. Shipping and the losses that come with it, including parcels that are seized or vanish. And margin, which is the only one of the five a buyer never sees.

Why the cheapest listing is not automatically suspicious

Because two of the five costs scale with volume, a large vendor can undercut a small one on identical material and still test every batch. Price alone therefore predicts less than intuition suggests: a low price can mean scale, or it can mean skipped testing, or it can mean a lower-grade raw material, and the number does not say which. The way to find out is not to compare prices; it is to compare the published testing behind each price. A vendor whose low price comes with a batch report on an independent laboratory's portal has explained its price. One whose low price comes with no report has not.

Why the most expensive listing is not automatically safer

Price signals margin as readily as it signals quality. Several vendors in this category charge a premium on the strength of their branding and publish less testing than cheaper competitors. The published record is again the discriminator: a premium that buys you a resolvable, batch-specific report from an accredited laboratory is a premium for something; a premium that buys a nicer website is not.

The comparison that actually works

Compare price per milligram, not per vial, because fill sizes differ and a 10 mg vial at twice the price of a 5 mg one is the same price. Then divide by what you can verify: a batch report you can open on the laboratory's domain, a stated purity from a named method, a shipping origin that does not cross a border. Two listings that survive that division can be compared on price. Two that do not are not comparable, however far apart their prices are, because one of them is a price for a known thing and the other is a price for a guess.

Sales, codes and the arithmetic of stacking

Percentage discounts applied in sequence multiply rather than add. A 15% sale followed by a 10% code comes to 23.5% off list, not 25%, because the code is applied to the already-reduced price. The difference is small on one vial and real on a large order, and it is the most common arithmetic mistake in coupon discussions. Buying tested product once still beats buying cheap product twice, and that comparison does not change with the size of the discount.
