The number alone answers nothing

A raw count of certificates is the least informative version of a testing claim. A seller with four products and sixty reports and a seller with four hundred products and sixty reports have published the same number while describing completely different operations — one has tested everything many times, the other has barely started.

The question that has a useful answer is not how many, but how many relative to what. Coverage and repetition are the two dimensions that matter, and both are computable from any published set in a few minutes.

A benchmark you can apply to anyone

Coverage first: how many distinct products does the set cover, against how many the catalogue actually sells? A set covering a third of what is on sale is telling you that two thirds of the shelf is undocumented, whatever the headline figure. Full coverage is the only level at which the count stops being a sample of the seller's choosing.

Repetition second: how many products appear more than once, under different batch numbers? This is the dimension almost nobody checks and the one that costs a seller real money. A single report describes one production run at one moment. Repeated reports across runs are what show whether quality held, which is the question a buyer actually has.

The reference set, counted

For a concrete reference point: one Canadian vendor's published record on October 6, 2026 holds 240 reports across 131 distinct products. Of those products, 41 appear more than once under different run numbers — roughly a third of the catalogue tested on more than one occasion, with one product line appearing twenty separate times.

Within the 240, 178 carry a numeric purity figure and 62 are identity or content tests. That split matters when comparing sets: quoting 240 as a count of purity reports would overstate the purity coverage by about a third. Separate the two before comparing anyone against anyone.

The gate that comes before any count

None of this arithmetic means anything if the documents cannot be verified. A large number of PDFs hosted on the seller's own server is not a large testing programme, because the party serving the file is the party with an interest in the number and can edit it.

So apply the gate first: do the reports open on a laboratory's own domain, under that laboratory's reference, naming the client and the batch? If yes, count them. If no, the count is a design decision rather than a measurement, and a bigger number simply means more design.

What a good set looks like when you stand back

Full or near-full catalogue coverage; a meaningful share of products tested more than once; both purity and identity represented; every report resolving on the laboratory's own system; and — the counter-intuitive one — a visible spread of results including some that are unremarkable.

That last criterion is the one most likely to be misread as a negative. A set where every published figure is excellent has more often been filtered than achieved, and the filtering is invisible from inside the set. Weak results left visible are evidence that you are reading a record rather than a selection, and they are what make the strong results worth anything.

One caution on applying all of this: the inference needs scale to work. Three published reports that are all excellent prove very little in either direction, and reading suspicion into a small set punishes a seller for being new rather than for being evasive. Coverage against catalogue size is the figure that survives at any scale, which is why it belongs first. The distribution argument only becomes available once there are enough results, across enough different compounds, that a spread could have appeared if one existed.
