The core idea: An independent lab and a vendor's own in-house testing are not the same thing. Independent testing — for example a widely used third-party lab like Janoshik — means a separate party ran the analysis. In-house means the seller graded its own homework.

What "independent" actually means

An independent result comes from a lab with no stake in the outcome. The value isn't the PDF itself — it's that the party running the test doesn't profit from a passing grade. That separation is the whole point.

Why in-house numbers deserve more scrutiny

When a seller tests its own product and publishes only the results it likes, you're seeing a curated picture, not an audit. This isn't an accusation against any specific company — it's a structural problem with self-reporting anywhere.

What to ask for

  • A COA that verifies on the testing lab's own portal.
  • The vendor named as the client on that report.
  • Ideally, independent blind testing — a buyer purchases off-the-shelf and submits it, so the sample can't be hand-picked.

What an in-house result is good for

In-house testing is not worthless; it is a different instrument answering a different question. A vendor that runs its own HPLC on every incoming batch is doing quality control, and quality control catches bad material before it ships. That is valuable to the vendor and indirectly to the buyer. What an in-house result cannot do is serve as evidence to an outsider, because the outsider has no way to know whether the instrument was calibrated, whether the sample was representative, or whether a failing result would have been published. The number may be honest; nothing about it lets you check.

What an independent result adds

Three things, and they are the three the in-house result lacks. A party with no stake produced it, so a failing result would have been reported to the client rather than deleted. It carries the laboratory's own reference and lives on the laboratory's site, so it can be looked up by anyone. And the laboratory's methods are its own, calibrated for many clients, so the number is comparable with the same laboratory's results for other vendors. Independence is not a claim of superior accuracy; it is a claim that the result exists outside the vendor's control, which is the property a buyer needs.

The middle case: independent lab, vendor-hosted PDF

Many vendors send samples to a genuine independent laboratory and then publish the result only as a PDF on their own site. The test was independent; the publication is not. The document may be entirely genuine, but a PDF can be edited, and a buyer cannot tell an edited one from an original by looking. The remedy is to take the report number to the laboratory's own lookup and see whether the same record comes back. If it does, the vendor-hosted copy was a convenience. If the laboratory has no such record, the copy is all there is.

What a widely used laboratory's portal tells you beyond the number

A laboratory that serves many vendors accumulates a record across the category, and its portal lets you see a vendor's results next to everyone else's on the same methods. That is where a vendor's archive becomes comparable: the same instrument, the same detector, the same reporting format. It is also where a vendor's claim to test “every batch” can be checked against the number of reports actually on file. An in-house programme, however diligent, offers no such comparison, because there is no one else on the same instrument.

What to ask a vendor that tests in-house

Not “is your testing good” but “would you send a batch to an outside laboratory and publish the reference”. The cost is small and the answer is informative either way.
