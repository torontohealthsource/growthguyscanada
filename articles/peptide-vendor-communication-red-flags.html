Short answer: How a vendor answers a simple testing question tells you more than its entire homepage. Evasiveness is the tell.

Green flags

A direct answer, a link to a COA that verifies on the lab's portal, the vendor named as the client, and a batch number that matches.

Red flags

Deflection, screenshots instead of verifiable links, in-house-only numbers, or pressure to buy before you can check anything.

The one question to ask

"Can you link the independent COA for this batch, verifiable on the lab's portal?" A confident vendor answers in one message. The rest reveal themselves.

The pattern behind the individual flags

Every communication red flag on a vendor list reduces to one question: does this seller answer a specific question with a specific answer? A legitimate operation can tell you which laboratory tested a batch, when, and where the report can be read, because those are facts it holds. An operation that does not hold them answers with reassurance instead — “all our products are tested”, “we use a top lab”, “trust us, thousands of happy customers”. The tell is not tone; it is the substitution of a general claim for the particular one you asked about.

Five exchanges worth having before a first order

Ask for the batch number and its report. The good answer is a batch identifier and a link that resolves on the laboratory's own site. A PDF sent as an attachment is weaker, because a file can be edited; a link on the lab's domain cannot.

Ask which laboratory, by name. Then look the laboratory up independently. A named lab with its own website, other clients and a way to verify a report number is the answer you want. A lab that exists only on the vendor's site is not a third party.

Ask where the order ships from. The honest answer is a country and, ideally, a city. Evasiveness here usually means the parcel crosses a border the vendor would rather not mention, which matters in Canada because that border is where orders in this category most often stall.

Ask what happens if a parcel does not arrive. A reship or refund policy stated in plain words, before you have paid, is a commitment. “We have never had a problem” is not.

Ask a question you already know the answer to. Something checkable from the public record — the lowest result they have published, the date of the most recent report. A vendor who answers accurately is reading its own record; one who answers with a better number than the record shows has just told you how it handles questions it cannot see.

Timing and channel tell you less than people think

Slow replies, a Telegram channel instead of an email address, or an informal register are weak signals. Small legitimate operations are slow and informal; large fraudulent ones are fast and polished. What survives contact with a real order is the content of the answers, not their speed or their polish, which is why the exchanges above are phrased as questions with checkable answers rather than as impressions.

The one flag that is close to decisive

Pressure to move the conversation or the payment somewhere it cannot be traced or reversed — a personal account, a crypto wallet presented as the only option after a card was offered, a request to “confirm” through a different app — is the flag most strongly associated with a parcel that never arrives. Unusual payment options on their own are common in this category and prove little; a last-minute change of channel is different in kind.
