A captive lab is a testing "lab" that exists to test for one vendor only — often spun up by, or alongside, that vendor. Because it serves a single master, its "independent" results are really the vendor grading its own homework. Independence is the entire value of third-party testing, and a captive lab has none.

Why it matters

The whole point of a third-party lab is that it has no stake in the result. A genuinely independent lab tests for many competing vendors — so it can't favour any one of them without losing the others. A captive lab has exactly one client to please, which removes the independence that made the testing meaningful in the first place.

How to recognize one

  • One client. Search the lab's name — if it only ever appears next to a single vendor, that's the tell.
  • Suspicious timing. A quick WHOIS lookup shows the lab's domain registered around the same time as the vendor's — sometimes days apart.
  • No footprint. No accreditation, no staff, no reviews, no presence outside that one vendor.
  • A "verify" portal that accepts anything. Type a made-up report number; if it returns a valid-looking result, it's theatre.

The contrast

A genuinely independent lab (used by hundreds of competing vendors, with a long track record and a real verification portal) is the opposite of captive. That independence — not a logo or a PDF — is what makes a result worth trusting.

How to recognise one

Captive laboratories are recognisable by what they lack. They have one client, or a small set of clients that share an owner. Their web domain was registered around the same time as the vendor's, often with the same registrar and privacy service. They have no listing in any accreditation body's directory and no proficiency-testing history. Their address, if given, is a mailbox or the vendor's own premises. Their reports resolve, if at all, on a portal that returns the same layout for any input. And they do not appear in the records of anyone who is not the vendor — no other vendor's certificates, no forum threads from buyers who used them independently, no history before the vendor needed one.

Why a captive laboratory is worse than no laboratory

A vendor with no testing is making no claim; a buyer knows where they stand. A vendor with a captive laboratory is making the claim of independence while holding both ends of it, and a buyer who takes the claim at face value has been given false confidence rather than none. The harm is not that the results are necessarily wrong — a captive laboratory may measure competently — but that no one outside the vendor can tell, and the whole value of a third party is that someone can.

The registration check

Domain registration records are public. Look up the laboratory's domain and the vendor's domain and compare the creation dates and registrant details. Two domains created within weeks of each other, with matching privacy services and hosting, are not proof of common ownership, but they are the single most common fingerprint of a captive arrangement, and a laboratory that predates the vendor by years is the opposite signal.

The client-list check

A real independent laboratory serves competitors. Search for the laboratory's name together with the names of other vendors in the category; if it appears on their certificates too, it is independent of each of them. If the only place it appears is one vendor's site, treat it as that vendor's department.

What to do when you find one

Treat the vendor as untested and apply the untested-vendor rules: ask for a report from a named independent laboratory, or look for a blind-test result from a programme that bought at retail. A vendor with good material has an easy path to demonstrating it; a vendor that built a laboratory instead of using one has chosen the harder path for a reason.
