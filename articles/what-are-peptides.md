> **What is a peptide, in one line?** A short chain of amino acids — the same building
blocks proteins are made of, just fewer of them (typically 2 to 50), linked together by
peptide bonds. That's a structural definition, not a claim about what any specific peptide
does.

## Peptides vs. proteins

The distinction is a rough convention, not a hard scientific line: shorter amino-acid chains
are usually called peptides, longer ones that often fold into complex 3D shapes are called
proteins. Insulin, for example, is sometimes described as one of the largest peptides or one of
the smallest proteins, depending on which textbook you read — the boundary is fuzzy by design.

## Research peptides vs. pharmaceutical peptides

The word "peptide" alone tells you nothing about legal or regulatory status. Some peptides
are approved medications — insulin and the GLP-1 class of drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide) are
peptides that went through full drug approval. Others are sold as research chemicals, labelled
"for research use only," without that approval process. The difference is in the specific
compound and how it's sold, not in the category "peptide" itself.

## FAQ

**What is a peptide?** A short chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds — typically 2 to 50 amino acids.

**How are peptides different from proteins?** Size and structure — peptides are shorter, proteins longer and often more complex. The line is a convention, not a hard rule.

**Are peptides drugs?** Some are approved pharmaceuticals (insulin, GLP-1 medications). Others are sold as research chemicals. It depends on the specific compound, not the category.
