Short answer: Independent identity and purity testing of a single sample is a modest flat fee at third-party labs — cheap relative to the order it protects. The number itself carries a lesson: if a buyer can afford to test, a vendor selling thousands of vials certainly can.

What you're paying for

A typical test bundle covers identity (is this the molecule it claims to be?) and purity (how much of the sample is that molecule?). Some buyers also pay for quantification. It's a small, predictable cost per sample.

Why "too expensive to test" is a red flag

When testing is this affordable, a vendor that skips it isn't saving money — it's avoiding scrutiny. The cost excuse doesn't hold up against the math of selling at scale.

How to submit a sample

  • Pick an independent lab, not one affiliated with the vendor.
  • Buy the product like a normal customer (a blind purchase).
  • Compare the result against the vendor's own published COA.

What a test actually costs

Independent laboratories that serve this category publish price lists, and the numbers are not large. A combined identity-and-purity analysis (HPLC purity plus mass confirmation) on a single peptide sample typically runs in the low hundreds of dollars. Adding a quantity assay adds a fraction of that. Sterility and endotoxin testing are priced separately and cost more, which is one reason they appear on far fewer reports. Turnaround is usually one to three weeks. None of this requires the vendor to do anything but ship a vial to the laboratory and pay the invoice.

What it costs per vial sold

The arithmetic that matters is the test cost divided by the batch size. A production batch of a few hundred vials tested once puts the cost of a report at well under a dollar per vial; a batch of a few thousand puts it at cents. Against a retail price of tens of dollars per vial, the testing line is a rounding error in the margin. A vendor that describes third-party testing as too expensive to do routinely is describing a batch size so small that the business itself is the surprising part, or is describing a preference.

What it costs a buyer to test independently

The same laboratories accept samples from individuals. Sending one vial for identity and purity costs the same low hundreds of dollars plus postage, and the report comes back in the buyer's name, which is the only form of evidence that is entirely outside the vendor's influence. It is not something most people will do for a routine order, but it is the reason community blind-testing programmes exist: a group shares the cost of testing a vendor's product bought at retail, and publishes the result. A vendor whose retail product has passed a blind test it did not arrange has cleared a higher bar than any certificate it commissioned.

Why the cost question is a red-flag test in itself

Because the cost is small and public, the reason a vendor gives for not testing is informative. “Our supplier tests it” means the vendor is relying on a document produced upstream, for a different party, about a batch it may not have received. “We test in-house” means the vendor is grading its own work. “Testing is too expensive” is arithmetically implausible at any commercial volume. The vendor that simply names a laboratory, a batch and a report has answered the question the others are avoiding, and it cost them less than a day's shipping.

What a report does not cost, and therefore does not prove

A report proves that one sample, on one day, had one composition. It does not prove the next batch, and it does not prove the vial in your hand unless the batch matches. The low cost of testing is exactly why per-batch testing is the reasonable expectation rather than a premium feature: a vendor can afford to do it every time, so the interesting question is whether it does.
