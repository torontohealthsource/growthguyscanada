What a certificate assumes about the sample it describes

A certificate describes the sample that reached the laboratory. Who selected that sample is the assumption behind every result, and it is almost never stated.

A result describes a sample, not a product

Every analytical result is a statement about the material that arrived at the laboratory. Extending it to a product line, a batch, or a vial in someone's hand requires an assumption: that the sample was representative and that it came from where it is said to have come from. That assumption is chain of custody, and it is usually invisible on the certificate.

Who selected the sample

If the seller chooses which vial to send, the result describes the vial the seller chose. That is not necessarily misleading — a random vial from a lot is a reasonable sample — but it is a different claim from one where the laboratory or a third party drew the sample. Certificates rarely state which happened, and the difference is material.

Why independent testing is a stronger claim

Independent here means the party commissioning the test is not the party selling the product. It removes the selection question entirely. It is also more expensive and much rarer, which is why most published results in this category are vendor-commissioned — a fact worth holding in mind rather than treating as disqualifying.

What the laboratory is and is not asserting

A laboratory asserts that it performed a stated method on a received sample and obtained a stated result. It does not assert that the sample was representative, that the client's description of its origin was accurate, or that other vials resemble it. Reading a certificate as a guarantee about a product line attributes to the laboratory a claim it did not make.

Where the batch code does real work

The code is the only field connecting a document to a physical object. A chain of custody you cannot inspect is at least anchored at one end if the code on your vial matches the code on a report hosted by the laboratory. That is a weaker guarantee than custody documentation and a much stronger one than a product name.

Lab-hosted versus seller-hosted, again

A report served by the seller leaves both ends of the chain in the same hands: the party choosing the sample is also the party serving the document. A report that resolves on the laboratory's own system removes the second half of that problem. It does not address the first, which is why 'independently hosted' and 'independently commissioned' are different claims and only one of them is common.

What a buyer can actually establish

Realistically: that a document exists, that it resolves on a laboratory's own domain, that it names the batch printed on your vial, and that the figures on it are what the seller reports them to be. That is a meaningful set of facts. It is not the same as knowing the sampling was unbiased, and the gap between them is worth stating rather than quietly closing.

Reading a published set with this in mind

Across many lots and a long period, selection effects become harder to sustain: consistently choosing favourable vials for dozens of tests is a different undertaking from doing it once. Volume and continuity therefore partly substitute for custody documentation — not perfectly, but enough that a long series deserves more weight than a short one, independent of how good the individual numbers look.
