# The four routes a compound breaks down by, and which ones you control

Four separate routes degrade a peptide — hydrolysis, oxidation, aggregation and adsorption — and reconstitution reopens all of them at once, which is why dry-state shelf life says nothing about the period after a diluent is added.

## Four routes, not one

Degradation is not a single process. Hydrolysis cleaves the peptide bond and needs water. Oxidation attacks specific residues and needs oxygen, often helped by trace metals or light. Aggregation brings molecules together into forms that no longer behave like the monomer. Adsorption simply loses material to the walls of the container. Each has different drivers, which is why a single instruction like 'keep it cold' is an incomplete answer.

## Why dry storage is the easy case

Removing water removes the main driver of hydrolysis and most of the medium the other routes operate in. That is why an unreconstituted vial tolerates ambient temperature for the length of a delivery and is comfortable in a fridge or freezer for far longer. The dry state is not inert, but it is slow enough that ordinary handling mistakes rarely matter over short periods.

## Why solution is the hard case

Once a diluent is added, every route reopens at once. The clock that was measured in months becomes one measured in days or weeks depending on the compound, the temperature, and whether the solution carries a preservative. Nothing written about the dry product's shelf life transfers across that boundary, and treating the two as one is the most common handling error in this category.

## Temperature is a rate, not a switch

Cold does not stop degradation; it slows it. The practical consequence is that brief excursions matter less than people fear and sustained warmth matters more than they expect. A vial that spent a day in transit at room temperature is in a different situation from one left on a shelf for a month, even though both were 'not refrigerated'.

## Freeze-thaw cycles

Repeated freezing and thawing of a solution is harder on a compound than either state alone, because each transition concentrates solutes and creates interfaces that drive aggregation. Where a solution must be kept, dividing it so that each portion is thawed once is the standard mitigation, and it is a handling decision rather than a property of the product.

## Light and the container

Several degradation routes are photochemical, which is why storage guidance so often specifies darkness as well as temperature. The container matters for a second reason: a very dilute solution can lose a meaningful fraction of its content to the vial wall by adsorption, which is a loss of quantity with no visible sign at all.

## What a certificate cannot cover

An analysis describes the material as it was at the laboratory on a date. Everything afterwards — transit, your storage, how long the vial waited before use — is outside the document. A strong result does not travel forward in time with the vial, which is the honest limit on what any report tells you about the thing in your hand today.

## Why 'refrigerate' is not a complete instruction

Temperature is only one of the four routes, and it is the one people remember because it is the easiest to act on. A vial kept cold but repeatedly opened, or kept cold in clear glass on a lit shelf, is being protected against one route while two others run unchecked. The useful form of the instruction names the state as well as the temperature: dry, sealed, cold and dark are four separate claims, and a product guidance line that gives only one of them has answered a quarter of the question.

## The short version

Keep the dry vial cool, dark and sealed. Treat reconstitution as the start of a much shorter clock. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw. Expect gradual loss rather than a cliff, and read a certificate as a statement about the past rather than a guarantee about the present.
