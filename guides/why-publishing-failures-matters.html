A clean set is either excellent or filtered

Any process that runs long enough produces an occasional poor result. A published set with none has either been unusually fortunate or has been selected. From the outside these look identical, which is precisely why the absence of weak results lowers rather than raises what the set can support.

The cost of publishing one is the point

A failure that is published is expensive to the publisher. That expense is what makes the rest of the record credible: a party willing to pay it when the result is bad is more plausibly reporting faithfully when the result is good. Nothing else on a page carries that information.

Volume without variance is the specific warning

A large published set with no spread is a stronger signal than a small one, because the larger the set, the less plausible that no result fell below the line. Size is usually presented as reassurance; combined with uniformity it is the opposite.

Look for the handling, not just the admission

A published failure followed by nothing is an admission. One followed by a stated action -- a lot withdrawn, a supplier changed, a retest -- describes a process. The second is considerably more informative about what happens the next time something goes wrong.

Check whether old entries survive

A record that only shows recent results has either been pruned or does not retain. Either way it cannot be used to see a trend, which is most of what a record is for. Look for whether entries from months ago are still reachable.

Corrections are the same signal in a different place

A publisher who leaves a corrected claim visible alongside the correction is paying the same cost in a different currency. The absence of any correction anywhere, over a long enough run, is not a sign that nothing was ever wrong.
