A listing makes a factual claim

A page that says a code gives a stated reduction is claiming something about the world that is either true or false at a given moment. That is unlike most marketing copy, which makes claims too vague to test. Being testable is the interesting property here, and it sets a standard the category mostly does not meet.

The cost of a wrong listing falls on the reader

A code that does not work costs a reader an abandoned cart and a few minutes. A code listed at a higher figure than it delivers costs them the difference, quietly, at the point of payment. Neither is catastrophic, which is precisely why the category tolerates so much of it.

Where the figure came from matters

A reduction can be read from a cart, read from a banner, or inferred from a description of the offer. Only the first is a measurement. Banners describe intentions and are often stale; descriptions are written by whoever benefits. Pages rarely say which of the three they used, and the difference is the difference between evidence and repetition.

Expiry is the usual failure

Most wrong listings were right once. Codes end, terms change, and a page that was accurate in March is a page that was accurate in March. Without a date the reader cannot tell a current claim from a historical one, and most listings carry no date at all or carry one that is generated rather than observed.

Aggregated listings inherit their sources

A page that lists twenty codes for a store has usually not tested twenty codes. It has collected them. Collection is a legitimate activity and produces a useful index, but it is not verification, and the distinction disappears when every entry is labelled the same way.

The incentive runs one way

Every party in the chain benefits from a listing that looks generous and current: the store, the publisher, and the reader who wants it to be true. Nobody in the chain is paid to find the error. Where incentives are one-directional, the published figure drifts in one direction too, and the correction has to be deliberate because it will not arrive on its own.

What a reader can check in thirty seconds

Whether the page states a date; whether it states what was in the cart; whether the figures are arithmetically consistent with each other. Those three take under a minute together and eliminate most of what is wrong on the internet about discounts.
