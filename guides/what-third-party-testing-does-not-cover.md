# The gap between a clean certificate and a safe order

## A certificate describes a sample, not a shipment

A laboratory reports on what arrived in its hands. It cannot speak to the other units in the batch, to how the material was handled afterwards, or to whether the box you receive was drawn from the lot that was tested. That is not a criticism of the laboratory; it is the boundary of what the document claims. A seller who presents a certificate as covering your order is extending it past its own wording.

## Identity and purity are separate questions

A purity figure says how much of the sample is the thing it was measured against. It does not independently establish that the thing is what the label says. Those are answered by different methods, and a report carrying one and not the other has answered half the question. Check which analyses the report actually lists rather than reading a high percentage as a general endorsement.

## Who chose the sample

Nearly every certificate in this category describes a sample the seller selected and sent. That is normal and it is not fraud, but it is a different thing from a sample drawn at random by the testing party. Blind or third-party-drawn sampling exists and is rare; where a report says which applied, that sentence is worth more than the purity figure above it.

## What a missing date on a report means

An analysis without a date cannot be tied to a production run. Two reports with the same figures and no dates may be the same test shown twice. The date, the lot reference and the laboratory's own identifier are what let a reader confirm a report belongs to the batch in front of them, and all three are routinely omitted.

## Repeat testing is where confidence comes from

One analysis of one sample constrains very little. A series of analyses across separate lots, published whether or not each was flattering, constrains a great deal. When assessing a seller, count how many reports exist and over what span rather than reading the best one closely.

## What to do with a report you cannot verify

Treat it as a claim rather than discarding it. An unverifiable report is weaker than a verifiable one and stronger than nothing, and the honest position is to say which of the three you are holding. Most disputes in this area come from treating the first category as the second.

## The useful question to ask a seller

Not 'is it tested' but 'can I see the report for this lot, and can I confirm it on the laboratory's own site'. A seller running a real testing programme answers both in a sentence. An answer that arrives as a summary rather than a document is itself informative.
