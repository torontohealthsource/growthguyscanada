The word 'verified' does almost no work on its own

The word 'verified' on a coupon page can mean four different things, and the page usually does not say which. When, against what, and what happened on failure are the questions that separate them.

Four things the word is used for

That someone once saw the code work. That an automated check ran. That a merchant supplied the code. Or that nobody checked anything and the word is decoration. The reader cannot tell these apart, and the page rarely says.

The question that separates them

When, against what, and what happened when it failed. A page that answers all three is making a different kind of claim from one that answers none, even though both use the same word.

What we mean by it here

A cart was built, the code entered at the cart stage, the discount line read back, and the result recorded whether it passed or not — 794 times, 792 of which returned a discount line. No order was ever submitted.

What this does not tell you

That our definition is the right one. It tells you what ours is, so you can discount it appropriately, which is more than an unqualified 'verified' allows.

The question to ask any coupon page

One question separates the four meanings: what would this page look like if the code had stopped working? If the answer is 'the same', the word 'verified' on it is decoration. If the answer is 'it would say so, with a date', the page is making a claim that can be wrong — which is the only kind worth reading.

The categories, defined

A useful status says what was done, not how confident someone feels. Applied means the code produced a discount line on a real cart on a stated date. Not applied means it was tried and produced nothing. Not tested means no cart was run, which is different from either and is the honest label for most of what gets published as 'verified'. Expired means an end date passed. Withdrawn means it worked and then stopped, which is the common case and the one most sites have no label for.

Why most sites have only one

A single undifferentiated 'verified' badge is cheaper to maintain and impossible to disprove, which is a combination that survives. Distinguishing between 'we tested it and it worked' and 'we have not tested it' requires actually testing, and publishing the second label requires admitting the gap. The categories above exist because the alternative is a page where everything carries the same word regardless of what stands behind it, and a reader cannot tell the checked claims from the assumed ones.

Why 'withdrawn' is the category that matters most

Codes rarely expire on an announced date. They are withdrawn, replaced, or quietly narrowed, and none of those events carries a warning. A site with only 'verified' and 'expired' has nowhere to put the most common outcome, so a withdrawn code sits under 'verified' until somebody notices — which, on an undated page, may be never. Asking whether a source distinguishes withdrawal from expiry is a quick way to tell whether it is tracking codes or just listing them.
