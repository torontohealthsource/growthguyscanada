Two percentage discounts on one order do not add. The second is taken from what the first left, so 10% and 20% come to 28%, not 30%.

The arithmetic

When two percentage reductions apply to one order, the second is calculated against what the first left behind, not against the original price. Ten percent and twenty percent do not make thirty. They make twenty-eight, because the twenty is taken from ninety.

Worked on a real cart

On the cart we read for this: $430.00 CAD subtotal. The code removes $43.00 CAD. The payment-method reduction then removes $77.40 CAD — calculated against the remainder, not the original — for $120.40 CAD off in total and $309.60 CAD to pay. That is 28%, not 30%.

Where else it applies

The same arithmetic governs a code stacked on a storewide sale. The sale price becomes the base, and the code comes off that — so the combined figure is always below the sum of the two rates, on every cart, without exception.

The same arithmetic, on a sale

A storewide sale and a code behave identically to two stacked percentages: the sale price becomes the base, and the code comes off that. So a 15% sale plus a 10% code is 23.5% off list, not 25%. This matters more than the Bitcoin case because sales are common and the overstatement is the kind a reader can verify in thirty seconds, which makes a page that gets it wrong easy to disbelieve on everything else.

A worked example, with real figures

On a cart measured October 6, 2026, a $430.00 CAD subtotal came to $309.60. A ten percent code took $43.00, leaving $387.00. A twenty percent payment-method reduction then applied to that $387.00 and took $77.40. Total removed: $120.40, which is 28% of the original. Adding the two percentages would have predicted 30% and $129.00 — a difference of $8.60 on one order. The error is small in isolation and compounds across a basket, and it is always in the same direction: the sum of the percentages overstates the saving.

The one case where they do add

Two reductions add rather than compound only when both are calculated against the same original figure. That happens when a store applies both to the pre-discount subtotal as separate line items, which is a deliberate configuration rather than the default. It is observable: if both lines are exactly their stated percentage of the original subtotal, they added; if the second is a percentage of what was left, they compounded. We have seen the same vendor change from one behaviour to the other within three weeks, which is why this is measured each day rather than assumed from the terms.

Why the error always runs the same way

Adding percentages always overstates the saving, never understates it, because the second reduction is applied to a smaller base than the first. That one-directional bias is what makes it worth knowing: an honest mistake in this arithmetic consistently flatters the offer. When a page quotes a combined figure that is exactly the sum of its parts, that is the signal to check it on a cart rather than to be impressed by it. The gap is small on one order and systematic across every comparison you make.
