A copper peptide whose literature is mostly topical and cosmetic, which is a different field from everything else in this index

This compound is studied largely in skin and wound contexts, with a body of work on topical application.

Its literature sits in dermatology, not metabolic research

This compound is studied largely in skin and wound contexts, with a body of work on topical application. That is a genuinely different field from the metabolic and repair literature surrounding most items here, with different endpoints, different measurement and different standards of evidence.

A copper complex is not a plain peptide

The molecule is a tripeptide bound to copper, and the copper is part of what is being studied rather than an impurity. That matters for how an assay is read: a purity figure describes the complex, and a report that does not state which form was measured has left out the thing that distinguishes it.

Topical evidence does not transfer to other routes

Most of the usable published work applies the compound to skin. Conclusions from that work say nothing about other routes of administration, and the step from one to the other is exactly where effect and safety would need to be re-established rather than assumed.

Why the assay figure here is narrow

The reports we hold establish identity and purity of the material supplied. In a field whose published outcomes are largely topical and cosmetic, that is a smaller claim than it might appear — it confirms the substance, not the use.

What a careful reader checks

Whether a cited study used the copper complex or the bare tripeptide, whether it was topical, and whether the endpoint was an observed outcome or a marker. Those three questions resolve most of the confusion in summaries about this compound.

The published record for this item

Topical and systemic evidence differ

Results from one route of administration say little about another, and summaries frequently merge them. Separating the two is the first step in reading this literature honestly.

Cosmetic studies have different endpoints

Work conducted in a cosmetic context often measures appearance-related outcomes over short periods. Those are legitimate findings for their own question and do not transfer to claims about other effects.
