Water is what degrades a peptide fastest, so the water is removed before shipping — which is also why a diluent is a separate purchase and why the vial can look empty.

Water is what degrades them

A peptide in solution is in the one environment where it breaks down fastest. Water participates directly in hydrolysis of the peptide bond, and it also carries the dissolved oxygen, trace metals and microbial load that drive the other degradation routes. Removing the water removes most of the chemistry at once, which is why a compound that is unstable for days in solution is stable for a long period dry.

What freeze-drying actually does

Lyophilisation freezes the solution, then lowers the pressure so the ice sublimes directly to vapour without passing through a liquid phase. The compound is left as a porous cake occupying roughly the shape of the frozen volume. The route matters: evaporating water with heat would expose the molecule to the temperatures it is least able to tolerate, which is the thing the process is designed to avoid.

Why the cake looks like almost nothing

A few milligrams of material spread through the volume of a vial produces a thin film or a barely visible disc, and it is routinely mistaken for an empty vial or for loss in transit. The visual has no relationship to the mass present. A cake that has shifted or collapsed into a smaller plug during shipping is a cosmetic change, not evidence of a problem.

Shipping without a cold chain

Because the dry form is comparatively stable, these products survive ordinary transit at ambient temperature for the period a domestic delivery takes. That is the practical reason the category can ship by ordinary post at all. It is also why the storage instructions before and after reconstitution are different documents in effect, and why reading only one of them causes problems.

Reconstitution starts a different clock

The moment a diluent is added, the stability argument above reverses. The compound is back in the environment the drying was meant to escape, so the handling constraints that did not apply to the shipped vial begin applying immediately. Anything written about shelf life in the dry state says nothing about the period after reconstitution.

Why the diluent is sold separately

The product ships dry because dry is how it survives; it therefore cannot ship pre-mixed, and the diluent is a separate purchase. This catches a large share of first orders, where compounds arrive and nothing can be done with them. The practical rule is to treat diluent as part of the order rather than an accessory to it.

What the dry form does not protect against

Freeze-drying slows hydrolysis; it does not stop oxidation, it does not protect against light, and it does not make a compound indifferent to temperature over long periods. Residual moisture left in the cake continues to matter. Dry means more stable, not inert, which is why storage guidance for the unreconstituted vial still specifies conditions rather than saying 'anywhere'.

What this means when you read a report

An analysis is performed on the material as it was at the laboratory, in whatever state the laboratory received it. It describes that sample at that moment. Everything that happened afterwards — transit, storage, how long the vial sat before you opened it — is outside the document, which is the honest limit of what any certificate can tell you about the thing in your hand.
